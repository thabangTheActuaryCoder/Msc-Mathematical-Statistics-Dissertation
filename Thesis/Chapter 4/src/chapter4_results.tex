\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This chapter presents the empirical results of the three-component
methodological framework specified in Chapter~\ref{chap:methods_and_data}.
The results are organised as follows:
Section~\ref{sec:hyperparameter_results} reports the sequential
hyperparameter comparison that produced the final \gls{dnn} training
configuration, together with the predictive performance of the retained
model and a baseline comparison with the actuarial-standard \gls{glm};
Section~\ref{sec:multiseed_dnn} presents the multi-seed \gls{dnn}
training robustness analysis across five random initialisations;
Section~\ref{sec:tuned_benchmarks} reports the tuned benchmark
comparison, including hyperparameter-optimised \gls{rf} and
\gls{xgboost} models;
Section~\ref{sec:multiseed_benchmark_stability} evaluates cross-seed
stability across all four model classes;
Section~\ref{sec:mc_results} presents the Monte Carlo
predicted-cost distribution, its joint-distribution input validation,
and convergence properties;
Section~\ref{sec:residual_augmented_results} evaluates the
residual-augmented sensitivity analysis;
Section~\ref{sec:shapley_results} reports the Shapley-value attribution
results at the global and high-cost conditional levels;
Section~\ref{sec:cross_seed_shap} presents the cross-seed Shapley
attribution stability analysis;
Section~\ref{sec:shap_interactions} reports the SHAP interaction
analysis; and
Section~\ref{sec:mc_shapley_integration} examines the correspondence
between the Monte Carlo distributional findings and the Shapley
attribution patterns.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Comparison Results}
\label{sec:hyperparameter_results}

The five-phase sequential hyperparameter comparison
(Section~\ref{sec:dnn_specification_training}) was carried out on the
architecture specified in Figure~\ref{fig:dnn_architecture_ch3}.
The results of each phase are presented below.

\subsubsection{Phase 1: Activation function}

Three candidate activation functions were compared: \gls{celu}
(equation~\eqref{eq:celu}), GELU and Tanh, with all other
hyperparameters fixed at learning rate $0.001$, batch size $256$ and Adam.

\noindent
The validation loss and $R^2$ trajectories for this phase are presented in
Figure~\ref{fig:phase1_activation_annex}
(Annexure~\ref{annexure:hyperparameters}).  \gls{celu} achieves the
lowest validation loss (approximately $0.00435$) and the highest
validation $R^2$ (approximately $0.9957$) from epoch~60 onwards.  GELU
tracks \gls{celu} closely but settles at a marginally higher loss
plateau.  Tanh converges more slowly and exhibits greater epoch-to-epoch
volatility, reaching a best validation $R^2$ of approximately $0.9954$.
The \gls{celu} activation was therefore retained.

\subsubsection{Phase 2: Learning rate}

Four learning rates were evaluated:
$\eta \in \{0.01, 0.001, 0.0005, 0.0001\}$, with \gls{celu} carried
forward.

\noindent
The validation trajectories are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:phase2_lr_annex}
(Annexure~\ref{annexure:hyperparameters}).
$\eta = 0.01$ produces catastrophic divergence: the validation loss
exceeds $1.0$ and the validation $R^2$ drops to near zero, indicating
that the gradient updates overshoot the loss surface at this step size.
The three remaining learning rates ($0.001$, $0.0005$ and $0.0001$) all
converge to validation $R^2 \approx 1.00$ on the standardised scale,
with their loss curves overlapping on the visible scale.  Among these,
$\eta = 0.0005$ achieves the lowest best validation loss and was
retained.

\subsubsection{Phase 3: Batch size}

Four batch sizes were compared:
$|\mathcal{B}| \in \{64, 128, 256, 512\}$, with \gls{celu} and
$\eta = 0.0005$ fixed.

\noindent
The validation trajectories are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:phase3_batchsize_annex}
(Annexure~\ref{annexure:hyperparameters}).
All four batch sizes converge to similar validation performance, with
best validation $R^2$ values between $0.9956$ and $0.9957$.  The batch
size $|\mathcal{B}| = 256$ was retained, as it achieves among the lowest
validation loss values while maintaining stable convergence behaviour.

\subsubsection{Phase 4: Optimiser}

Adam and NAdam were compared with all previous best choices carried
forward.

\noindent
The validation trajectories are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:phase4_optimiser_annex}
(Annexure~\ref{annexure:hyperparameters}).
NAdam converges faster than Adam in the initial epochs and exhibits
smoother validation loss trajectories throughout training.  From
approximately epoch~40 onwards, NAdam consistently achieves lower
validation loss and higher validation $R^2$ than Adam, with fewer loss
spikes.  NAdam was therefore retained.

\subsubsection{Phase 5: Momentum parameters}

Three momentum configurations were compared for the NAdam optimiser:
$(\beta_1, \beta_2) \in \{(0.90, 0.999),\; (0.95, 0.999),\;
(0.99, 0.999)\}$.

\noindent
The validation trajectories are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:phase5_momentum_annex}
(Annexure~\ref{annexure:hyperparameters}).
All three momentum configurations converge to similar final validation
performance.  The $\beta_1 = 0.95$ configuration achieves marginally
lower and more stable validation loss from epoch~40 onwards, with the
best validation $R^2$ reaching approximately $0.9957$.  The conservative
setting $\beta_1 = 0.99$ converges slightly faster in early epochs but
exhibits comparable final performance.  The configuration
$(\beta_1, \beta_2) = (0.95, 0.999)$ was retained.

Figure~\ref{fig:validation_loss_overview_annex}

\noindent
The \gls{dnn} outperforms the \gls{glm} on every metric.  On the test

$0.9571$ for the \gls{glm}, an improvement of $3.86$ percentage points.
The test \gls{rmse} decreases from R913 to R290 (a $68.2\%$ reduction),
and the test \gls{mae} decreases from R624 to R251 (a $59.8\%$
reduction).

The two tree-based benchmarks narrow the gap considerably.  The \gls{rf}
achieves a test $R^2$ of $0.9899$, reducing the \gls{glm}'s test
\gls{rmse} from R913 to R443 ($51.5\%$) and its \gls{mae} from R624 to
R358 ($42.6\%$).  However, its training $R^2$ of $0.9946$ versus its
test $R^2$ of $0.9899$ indicates a degree of overfitting despite the
\texttt{max\_depth}~$= 30$ constraint, suggesting that the ensemble
memorises some training-set noise.  \gls{xgboost} performs substantially
better, achieving a test $R^2$ of $0.9955$ with a test \gls{rmse} of
R297 and a test \gls{mae} of R255.  Its training-to-test gap is
negligible ($R^2_{\mathrm{train}} = 0.9959$ versus
$R^2_{\mathrm{test}} = 0.9955$), indicating effective regularisation
from the learning rate ($\eta = 0.05$) and subsampling parameters.
The \gls{dnn} marginally outperforms \gls{xgboost} on all three test metrics
($R^2$: $0.9957$ versus $0.9955$; \gls{rmse}: R290 versus R297;
\gls{mae}: R251 versus R255), while showing perfect
training-to-test agreement.  The \gls{dnn}'s advantage over the
tree-based models, although modest in absolute terms, is consistent
with its ability to learn smooth, continuous mappings through
backpropagation, whereas tree-based models approximate these mappings
through piecewise-constant splits.

\noindent
Figure~\ref{fig:four_model_comparison_ch4} visualises the test-set
comparison across all four model classes.  The \gls{glm}'s inferior fit
is attributable to the log-link's multiplicative mean structure, which
does not match the additive data-generating process underlying this
dataset.  The charges variable is constructed as an approximately linear
function of the input features plus noise; the \gls{glm}'s exponential
inverse-link therefore introduces systematic model misspecification.
The \gls{rf} and \gls{xgboost} close the gap substantially, with \gls{xgboost}
nearly matching the \gls{dnn}.  The \gls{dnn} learns the additive
structure directly through its flexible hidden-layer representations and
achieves the highest test $R^2$ and lowest test RMSE and MAE.

\caption{Overfitting diagnostic: training versus test $R^2$ for all

\label{fig:train_test_r2_overfitting_ch4}
\end{figure}

\noindent
Figure~\ref{fig:train_test_r2_overfitting_ch4} compares the training
and test $R^2$ for all four models.  The \gls{glm} shows negligible
generalisation loss ($R^2_{\mathrm{train}} = 0.9574$ versus
$R^2_{\mathrm{test}} = 0.9571$), as expected for a parametric model
with far fewer parameters than observations.  The \gls{rf} exhibits the
largest train-test gap ($R^2_{\mathrm{train}} = 0.9946$ versus
$R^2_{\mathrm{test}} = 0.9899$), indicating a degree of overfitting
despite the depth constraint.  \gls{xgboost} shows a negligible gap
($R^2_{\mathrm{train}} = 0.9959$ versus
$R^2_{\mathrm{test}} = 0.9955$), confirming that the learning rate and
subsampling regularisation are effective.  The \gls{dnn} likewise shows
no evidence of overfitting ($R^2_{\mathrm{train}} = 0.9957$ versus
$R^2_{\mathrm{test}} = 0.9957$), confirming that the early stopping
criterion (Section~\ref{sec:dnn_specification_training}) effectively
regularises the 49\,168 trainable parameters.

The benchmark comparison serves two purposes.  First, it establishes
that the \gls{dnn}'s high $R^2$ reflects genuine predictive superiority
over the actuarial-standard \gls{glm} and competitive performance
relative to the tree-based alternatives, not merely an artefact of the
dataset's synthetic regularity.  Second, because the Gamma/log-link
\gls{glm} is the model an actuary would deploy as a first approach and
\gls{xgboost} represents the current state of the art for tabular data,
the comparison provides practical benchmark context that situates the
\gls{dnn} within the actuarial modelling landscape.  The \gls{dnn} is
therefore retained for the subsequent analyses on the grounds of its
superior accuracy and its differentiability for gradient-based
attribution.

The finding that a deep neural network outperforms a classical \gls{glm}
on this benchmark is consistent with the broader health-cost prediction
literature.  \citet{dreweboss2022deep} report that deep-learning models
achieve lower prediction error than standard actuarial models on claims
data, while \citet{samiuddin2023health} and \citet{kaushik2022machine}
similarly observe that neural-network architectures improve upon
traditional regression baselines for insurance cost estimation.  As noted
in Section~\ref{sec:integrating_dnn_mc_shapley}, however, this
superiority is not universal across all tabular insurance datasets
\citep{richman2021ai}, and the present comparison is conducted on a
benchmark dataset with a strong synthetic structure.

A related line of actuarial research applies explicit shape constraints
to neural networks during training.  \citet{richman2024icenet} develop
the ICEnet, which adds smoothness and monotonicity penalties to the loss
function so that Individual Conditional Expectation outputs remain
actuarially reasonable.  Because that study targets claims frequency
under a Poisson deviance criterion rather than continuous monetary costs,
a direct numerical comparison with the present results is not possible;
the methodological differences are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:comparison_constrained_nn}.

\subsection{Multi-Seed DNN Training Robustness}
\label{sec:multiseed_dnn}

The results reported above are conditional on a single trained network
with a fixed random seed (seed~42).  To assess the sensitivity of the
predictive performance and subsequent analyses to the stochastic
training process, the FunnelDNN was retrained from scratch under five
independent random seeds (42, 123, 456, 789 and 1024), each producing a
different weight initialisation and mini-batch ordering.  All other
training settings (architecture, learning rate, batch size, optimiser,
momentum and early stopping criterion) were held fixed at the values in
Table~\ref{tab:final_hyperparameters_ch4}.

\subsubsection{Training convergence across seeds}

The training loss, validation loss and validation $R^2$ trajectories
across all five seeds are presented in
Annexure~\ref{annexure:multiseed_training}
(Figures~\ref{fig:dnn_multiseed_train_loss_annex}
to~\ref{fig:dnn_multiseed_epoch_vs_r2_annex}).  All five seeds converge
to the same loss plateau, with the validation $R^2$ trajectories
virtually indistinguishable from epoch~60 onwards.  No seed is trapped
in a materially different local minimum, confirming that the optimisation
landscape for this architecture and dataset is well-behaved.

\subsection{Monte Carlo Predicted-Cost Distribution}
\label{sec:mc_results}

The base parametric Monte Carlo simulation (Algorithm~3.1) was executed
with $B = 100\,000$ synthetic policyholder profiles drawn from the
product-marginal input distribution specified in
equation~\eqref{eq:raw_product_mc_distribution_ch3}. Each simulated
profile was preprocessed, scored through the fitted \gls{dnn}, and

expected under independence
(Figure~\ref{fig:mc_joint_continuous_pairs_annex}).  Conditional

independence among the training-data categoricals

occupy novel regions of the feature space.

The marginal distributions of all 11 raw predictor variables closely
match the training-data marginals
(Figure~\ref{fig:mc_input_distributions_annex}).  The parametric
simulation therefore faithfully implements the distributional assumptions
stated in Table~\ref{tab:mc_distribution_assumptions_ch3}, at both the

$1\,000$ observations determine the percentile estimate, compared with
$5\,000$ at $\tau_{95}$.  The running 99th percentile has stabilised to
within $\pm 0.4\%$ of its final value from $B = 25\,000$ onwards,
indicating that the tail percentile estimates are sufficiently precise
for the conditional Shapley analysis that follows.

same value for virtually all members of a conditioning set necessarily
contributes a near-constant Shapley attribution.  The convergence of
these four independent lines of evidence supports the conclusion that
the ranking reversal reflects a genuine structural property of the
fitted model's attribution surface, not a finite-sample artefact.

\label{fig:shap_threshold_comparison_ch4}
\end{figure}

\noindent
Figure~\ref{fig:shap_threshold_comparison_ch4} displays the
threshold-comparison bar chart. The dominant pattern is a monotonic
increase in the conditional importance of
\texttt{coverage\_level}, \texttt{medical\_history} and
\texttt{family\_medical\_history} as the threshold moves from the global
level through $\tau_{90}$ and $\tau_{95}$ to $\tau_{99}$. In contrast,
\texttt{smoker} shows a marked decline in conditional importance at the
most extreme threshold ($0.563$ at $\tau_{99}$ versus $0.578$ globally).
This decline does not indicate that smoking status becomes
unimportant; rather, it reflects a ceiling effect: nearly all profiles
in the extreme upper tail are smokers (as confirmed in
Figure~\ref{fig:highcost_profiles_ch4} below), so the binary variable
contributes a near-constant positive attribution with reduced
variability, while the remaining features differentiate among the
high-cost profiles.

The second tier of variables (\texttt{occupation},
\texttt{exercise\_frequency}, \texttt{gender}, \texttt{bmi},
\texttt{children}, \texttt{age} and \texttt{region}) remains stable
across all thresholds, with conditional importance values close to
their global values. This stability indicates that these variables
contribute a consistent baseline attribution regardless of the cost
region.

The ranking reversal at $\tau_{99}$ directly addresses the research gap
identified in Section~\ref{sec:research_gap}: existing health-cost
prediction studies typically report global feature importance on observed
data only \citep{kaushik2022machine, rozemberczki2022shapley}, without
conditioning attributions on specific regions of the predicted-cost
distribution.  The conditional Shapley analysis demonstrates that global
importance rankings can be misleading when the interest lies in the
upper tail, because a variable that dominates on average (smoking status)
may lose its discriminating power within an extreme subpopulation where
it is nearly constant.  This distinction has not been documented in the
health insurance cost-prediction literature reviewed in
Chapter~\ref{sec:literature_review}.

\subsubsection{High-cost profile characteristics}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{Thesis/Chapter 4/Figures/fig_highcost_profile_comparison.png}
\caption{Distributional profiles of the high-cost sets compared with the full Monte Carlo sample.}
\label{fig:highcost_profiles_ch4}
\end{figure}

\noindent
Figure~\ref{fig:highcost_profiles_ch4} profiles the composition of the
high-cost sets at each threshold. The categorical panels reveal a
progressive concentration:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Smoker:} The proportion of smokers increases from
    approximately $50\%$ in the full Monte Carlo sample to approximately
    $93\%$ at $\tau_{90}$, $96\%$ at $\tau_{95}$ and $99\%$ at
    $\tau_{99}$. At the most extreme threshold, virtually all high-cost
    profiles are smokers.

    \item \textbf{Coverage level:} The proportion of Premium-tier
    policyholders rises from approximately $33\%$ (full MC) to $75\%$
    at $\tau_{90}$, $80\%$ at $\tau_{95}$ and approximately $90\%$ at
    $\tau_{99}$. Basic-tier policyholders are nearly absent from the
    extreme tail.

    \item \textbf{Medical history:} Heart disease dominates the
    high-cost sets: its proportion increases from approximately $25\%$
    (full MC) to $68\%$ at $\tau_{90}$, $77\%$ at $\tau_{95}$ and
    approximately $85\%$ at $\tau_{99}$. Profiles with no medical
    history condition are progressively excluded from the upper tail.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The continuous variables (\texttt{age}, \texttt{bmi},
\texttt{children}) show little change in their distributional shapes
across thresholds, consistent with their small and stable conditional
Shapley values. This confirms that the high-cost region is defined
primarily by the categorical risk factors rather than the continuous
covariates.

\subsubsection{Representative high-cost waterfall decomposition}

A representative high-cost profile at the $\tau_{95}$ threshold
(predicted cost R24\,249) was decomposed using a SHAP waterfall plot
(Figure~\ref{fig:shap_waterfall_annex},
Annexure~\ref{annexure:shap_waterfall}).  The profile is characterised
by age~$= 34$, \texttt{bmi}~$= 31.4$, \texttt{children}~$= 0$,
\texttt{smoker}~$=$ yes, \texttt{family\_medical\_history}~$=$ Heart
disease, \texttt{medical\_history}~$=$ Heart disease and
\texttt{coverage\_level}~$=$ Basic.

The prediction is driven upward from the baseline
($E[f(X)] = -0.044$) to the final prediction ($f(x) = 1.699$)
primarily by \texttt{medical\_history} ($+0.67$),
\texttt{family\_medical\_history} ($+0.67$) and \texttt{smoker}
($+0.59$), partially offset by \texttt{coverage\_level} ($-0.51$).
The remaining variables contribute smaller amounts, with the continuous
covariates exerting modest negative effects (\texttt{bmi}: $-0.04$;
\texttt{age}: $-0.03$; \texttt{region}: $-0.01$).  This waterfall
illustrates the additive decomposition guaranteed by the Shapley
efficiency axiom: the contributions sum exactly to the difference
between the prediction and the baseline, as required by
equation~\eqref{eq:shapley_decomp}.

\subsection{Cross-Seed Shapley Attribution Stability}
\label{sec:cross_seed_shap}

The Shapley-value attributions were recomputed for all $B = 100\,000$
Monte Carlo profiles using each of the five trained networks from
Section~\ref{sec:multiseed_dnn}.  This analysis directly addresses the
limitation identified in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations_conclusion}: whether the Shapley
attributions are sensitive to the stochastic training process.

\subsubsection{Global SHAP stability}

The global mean $|\text{SHAP}|$ values were computed for each seed and
compared across all ten pairwise seed combinations.  Detailed
visualisations of the seed-level importance values and cross-seed
dispersion are presented in
Annexure~\ref{annexure:multiseed_shap}
(Figures~\ref{fig:shap_seed_stability_annex}
and~\ref{fig:shap_cross_seed_dispersion_annex}).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Thesis/Chapter 4/Figures/fig_shap_spearman_heatmap.png}
\caption{Spearman rank-correlation heatmap of the global mean
$|\text{SHAP}|$ importance vectors across all ten pairwise seed
combinations.  Every cell equals $\rho_{\mathrm{S}} = 1.0000$.}
\label{fig:shap_spearman_heatmap_ch4}
\end{figure}

\noindent
Figure~\ref{fig:shap_spearman_heatmap_ch4} displays the Spearman
rank-correlation matrix across all ten pairwise seed combinations.
Every off-diagonal cell equals $\rho_{\mathrm{S}} = 1.0000$: the global
feature-importance ranking is perfectly preserved across all five seeds.
The coefficient of variation of the mean $|\text{SHAP}|$ values is below
$0.1\%$ for every variable
(Figure~\ref{fig:shap_cross_seed_dispersion_annex}), confirming that the
magnitude of the attributions, not merely the ranking, is stable across
seeds.

\subsubsection{Conditional SHAP stability at $\tau_{99}$}

The conditional Shapley analysis at the $\tau_{99}$ threshold was
repeated for each seed to assess whether the ranking reversal documented
in Section~\ref{sec:highcost_conditional_results} is reproducible.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Thesis/Chapter 4/Figures/fig_cond_shap_tail_stability.png}
\caption{Conditional mean $|\text{SHAP}|$ values at $\tau_{99}$ across

\label{fig:cond_shap_tail_stability_ch4}
\end{figure}

\noindent
Figure~\ref{fig:cond_shap_tail_stability_ch4} confirms that the
$\tau_{99}$ ranking reversal is replicated in all five seeds:
\texttt{family\_medical\_history} and \texttt{medical\_history}
consistently overtake \texttt{smoker} at the extreme upper tail.  The
pairwise Spearman correlations of the conditional importance vectors
range from $\rho_{\mathrm{S}} = 0.9909$ to $1.0000$ across seed pairs,
with minor rank swaps occurring only between adjacent variables with
nearly identical importance magnitudes.  This result establishes that the
ranking reversal is a robust structural property of the architecture and
data, not an artefact of a single random initialisation.

\subsubsection{SHAP dependence patterns}

The SHAP dependence plots for the \texttt{smoker} variable and the
\texttt{bmi}/\texttt{coverage\_level} relationship are presented in
Annexure~\ref{annexure:multiseed_dependence}
(Figures~\ref{fig:shap_dependence_smoker_annex}
and~\ref{fig:shap_dependence_bmi_coverage_annex}).  The binary
\texttt{smoker} variable produces a clear bimodal attribution pattern
that is replicated across all five seeds.  The
BMI-SHAP relationship is near-perfectly linear, and no smoker-BMI
interaction is evident, consistent with the additive structure of the
benchmark dataset.

\subsection{SHAP Interaction Analysis}
\label{sec:shap_interactions}

To assess whether the DNN has learned meaningful pairwise interactions
among the input variables, the SHAP interaction structure was analysed by
computing the pairwise Pearson correlations between all
$\binom{11}{2} = 55$ pairs of raw-variable Shapley-value vectors across
the $B = 100\,000$ Monte Carlo profiles.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Thesis/Chapter 4/Figures/fig_shap_interaction_heatmap.png}
\caption{SHAP interaction heatmap: pairwise Pearson correlations between
the Shapley-value vectors of all 11 raw variables.  All off-diagonal
correlations fall below $|r| = 0.033$.}
\label{fig:shap_interaction_heatmap_ch4}
\end{figure}

\noindent
Figure~\ref{fig:shap_interaction_heatmap_ch4} displays the pairwise
interaction heatmap.  All 55 off-diagonal correlations fall below
$|r| = 0.033$, indicating that the Shapley contributions of different
variables are essentially uncorrelated across profiles.  The top 15
pairwise correlations, visualised in
Figure~\ref{fig:shap_interaction_pairs_annex}
(Annexure~\ref{annexure:multiseed_interactions}), confirm that no pair
exhibits a correlation exceeding $|r| = 0.033$.

This finding has two implications.  First, it confirms that the DNN has
learned an essentially additive mapping from the input features to the
predicted cost, consistent with the additive data-generating process of
the benchmark dataset.  Second, it validates the use of main-effect
Shapley summaries (global mean $|\text{SHAP}|$ and conditional mean
$|\text{SHAP}|$) as adequate characterisations of the model's
attribution structure: because pairwise interactions are negligible, the
main-effect Shapley values capture the full attribution pattern without
material loss of information.

\subsection{Integration of Monte Carlo and Shapley Results}
\label{sec:mc_shapley_integration}

The Monte Carlo and Shapley analyses provide complementary perspectives
on the fitted \gls{dnn}'s behaviour. The Monte Carlo simulation
identifies the distributional regions of interest within the
predicted-cost landscape, while the Shapley-value decomposition reveals
which input variables are associated with those regions.

The Monte Carlo predicted-cost distribution
(Figure~\ref{fig:mc_predicted_cost_distribution_ch4}) is approximately
symmetric with a mean of R16\,752 and an upper tail extending to
R31\,781. Within this landscape, three quantile-based thresholds
define high-cost regions of increasing severity:
$u_{0.90} = \text{R22\,585}$, $u_{0.95} = \text{R24\,249}$ and
$u_{0.99} = \text{R27\,049}$.

The global Shapley analysis
(Figure~\ref{fig:shap_global_importance_ch4}) identifies
\texttt{smoker} as the dominant driver of fitted predictions across
the full simulated input space. However, the conditional analysis
(Figures~\ref{fig:shap_conditional_importance_ch4}
and~\ref{fig:shap_threshold_comparison_ch4}) reveals that the
attribution structure changes in the upper tail. As the high-cost
threshold becomes more stringent, the conditional importance of
\texttt{coverage\_level}, \texttt{medical\_history} and
\texttt{family\_medical\_history} increases monotonically, while the
importance of \texttt{smoker} declines slightly.

The profile-level analysis
(Figure~\ref{fig:highcost_profiles_ch4}) explains this
re-ordering: at $\tau_{99}$, virtually all high-cost profiles are
smokers ($99\%$), so smoking status no longer differentiates among
profiles within the extreme tail. The variables that differentiate
high-cost profiles from one another are therefore coverage tier,
personal medical history and family medical history. At $\tau_{99}$,
approximately $90\%$ of high-cost profiles hold Premium coverage and
$85\%$ report Heart disease. These three variables collectively define
the archetype of the extreme upper-tail profile.

The continuous covariates (\texttt{age}, \texttt{bmi},
\texttt{children}) play a limited role at all threshold levels. Their
conditional importance values remain close to their global values, and
their distributional shapes within the high-cost sets are not
meaningfully different from those in the full Monte Carlo sample. The
fitted \gls{dnn} therefore assigns the dominant cost-driving role to
the categorical risk factors, with the continuous variables contributing
a modest, stable baseline effect.

The invariance checks reinforce the robustness of these findings. The
Shapley attributions are stable whether computed on training data or
Monte Carlo profiles (Spearman $\rho = 1.000$,
Figure~\ref{fig:shap_training_vs_mc_invariance_annex}), and the
residual-augmented sensitivity analysis
(Figure~\ref{fig:mc_residual_augmented_ch4}) confirms that the
predicted-cost distribution is not sensitive to fitted residual
variability. The attribution patterns therefore reflect the fitted
model's learned internal structure rather than artefacts of the input
regime or residual noise.

The multi-seed robustness analyses of
Sections~\ref{sec:multiseed_dnn} to~\ref{sec:shap_interactions} provide a
further layer of validation.  The DNN's test $R^2$ varies by only
$1.28 \times 10^{-5}$ across five random seeds
(Table~\ref{tab:multiseed_test_performance_ch4}); the Monte Carlo mean
predicted cost varies by R15 (Section~\ref{sec:cross_seed_mc_ch4}); and
the global Shapley importance ranking is perfectly preserved across all
ten pairwise seed combinations ($\rho_{\mathrm{S}} = 1.0000$,
Figure~\ref{fig:shap_spearman_heatmap_ch4}).  The $\tau_{99}$ ranking
reversal is replicated in all five seeds
(Figure~\ref{fig:cond_shap_tail_stability_ch4}), and the SHAP
interaction analysis confirms that the DNN has learned an essentially
additive mapping (all pairwise correlations below $|r| = 0.033$,
Figure~\ref{fig:shap_interaction_heatmap_ch4}).  These convergent
findings establish that the entire chain of results, from predictive
performance through the Monte Carlo distribution to the global and
conditional Shapley attributions, is invariant to the stochastic training
process.

These results are summarised, evaluated and situated within the broader
context of the study in Chapter~\ref{sec:conclusion}.
