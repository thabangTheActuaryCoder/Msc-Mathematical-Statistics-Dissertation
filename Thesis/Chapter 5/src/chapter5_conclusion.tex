% 5.1 REVIEW OF THE DISSERTATION
% ---------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Review of the Dissertation}
\label{sec:summary_of_findings}

Health insurance cost prediction is, at its core, a regression problem:
the analyst seeks to estimate a continuous monetary outcome from a vector
of policyholder characteristics.  In the actuarial profession, this
estimation has traditionally been performed using \glspl{glm}, and in particular the Gamma distribution with a logarithmic link,
which imposes a multiplicative mean structure on the relationship between
covariates and costs.  The appeal of this classical framework lies in its
interpretability: each coefficient describes the proportional change in
the conditional mean cost associated with a unit change in a predictor,
holding all other predictors fixed.  This dissertation began by asking
whether a more flexible model class, specifically \glspl{dnn},
could improve upon this classical baseline, and whether the improved
predictions could be analysed through methods that go beyond
point-prediction accuracy.

The motivation for moving beyond point prediction was developed in
Chapter~\ref{sec:introduction}, which argued that a fitted prediction
model is a statistical object whose properties merit systematic
investigation.  Just as the sampling distribution of an estimator is
studied through \gls{mc} experiments to understand its variability and
tail behaviour, the distribution of a prediction function's outputs under
a specified input law can be studied to understand the range, shape and
extreme-value behaviour of its predictions.  And just as the contribution
of individual observations to an estimator can be decomposed through
influence functions, the contribution of individual input variables to a
prediction can be decomposed through Shapley values.  These parallels
between classical statistical methodology and modern machine-learning
interpretation formed the conceptual basis of the dissertation.

The literature review in Chapter~\ref{sec:literature_review} established
the theoretical and empirical foundations for each component of the
framework, surveying supervised regression, health insurance pricing,
\gls{mc} simulation and Shapley-value attribution in their respective
traditions.  The review identified the research gap: the existing
literature applies deep learning and Shapley-value methods in isolation,
without connecting the distributional analysis of a model-implied
predicted-cost landscape to conditional feature-importance attribution
within specific regions of that landscape.

Chapter~\ref{chap:methods_and_data} translated the conceptual framework
into a concrete empirical workflow applied to the Kaggle Insurance Data
for Machine Learning benchmark dataset \citep{kaggleInsuranceDataML},
containing one million policyholder records.  The chapter specified the
data preprocessing, the \gls{dnn} architecture and five-phase hyperparameter
comparison, the three benchmark models (Gamma \gls{glm}, \gls{rf},
XGBoost), the \gls{mc} simulation design and the validation
diagnostics (see Table~\ref{tab:modelling_assumptions_ch3} for the
consolidated modelling assumptions).

The empirical results in Chapter~\ref{sec:results} demonstrated the
value of the integrated framework.  The five-phase sequential
hyperparameter comparison selected the final \gls{dnn} configuration, which
achieved test-set $R^2 = 0.9957$ with near-identical training and test
metrics ruling out overfitting.  The \gls{dnn} substantially outperformed the
Gamma \gls{glm}, whose log-link multiplicative structure does not match the
approximately additive data-generating process of the benchmark data.
The tree-based benchmarks (\gls{rf} and XGBoost) also achieved
strong performance; after tuning, XGBoost matched the \gls{dnn} to four
decimal places.  The \gls{dnn} was retained for the subsequent analyses on the
grounds of its differentiability for gradient-based SHAP computation.
Full performance comparisons are reported in
Chapter~\ref{sec:results}.

The \gls{mc} simulation produced a predicted-cost distribution whose
summary statistics closely match the empirical charge distribution, as
expected given the near-perfect fit and the use of training-data
marginals (see the circularity caveat in
Section~\ref{sec:mc_intro}).  The joint-distribution validation
confirmed that the product-marginal input does not introduce material
distributional distortion, and the residual-augmented sensitivity
analysis established robustness to fitted residual perturbation.

The conditional Shapley analysis produced the most distinctive finding.
At $\tau_{99}$, the global feature-importance ranking reverses: family
medical history, medical history and coverage level overtake smoking
status.  This reversal is explained by the compositional structure of
the extreme upper tail, where virtually all profiles are smokers and
smoking status therefore loses discriminating power (the mechanical and
substantive components of this reversal are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:addressing_rqs}).  The multi-seed robustness analysis
confirmed that the entire chain of results, including the $\tau_{99}$
ranking reversal, is invariant to the stochastic training process across
five independent seeds (details in Chapter~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Addressing the Research Questions}
\label{sec:addressing_rqs}

The six research questions posed in Section~\ref{sec:research_questions}
span the predictive, simulation and explanation stages of the framework.
Each question is now addressed in light of the empirical results.

\subsubsection*{Research Question~\ref{rq:1}}
How does a \gls{dnn} regression model compare with a
classical \gls{glm}, a \gls{rf} and an XGBoost
gradient-boosted tree in predicting health insurance costs on a benchmark
dataset?

The \gls{dnn} achieves a test-set \gls{r2} of $0.9957$, a test \gls{mae} of R251 and a
test \gls{rmse} of R290.  The Gamma \gls{glm} (log-link) achieves a test-set \gls{r2} of
$0.9571$, a test \gls{mae} of R624 and a test \gls{rmse} of R913.  The \gls{dnn} therefore
outperforms the \gls{glm} on every metric, reducing the test \gls{rmse} by $68.2\%$
and the test \gls{mae} by $59.8\%$.  The two tree-based benchmarks narrow the
gap: the \gls{rf} achieves $R^2 = 0.9899$ and XGBoost reaches
$R^2 = 0.9955$, approaching the \gls{dnn}'s accuracy.  All four models show
near-identical training and test performance, ruling out overfitting.
The \gls{glm}'s performance gap is attributable to the log-link's
multiplicative mean structure, which introduces systematic model
misspecification on this benchmark dataset where the charges variable is
constructed as an approximately additive function of the input features.
The tree-based models, which are not constrained by a multiplicative
link, recover most of this gap, confirming that the \gls{glm}'s disadvantage
is structural rather than a general property of simpler model classes.
The five-phase sequential hyperparameter comparison selected the final
\gls{dnn} configuration from among 16 candidate settings, documented in full
in Annexure~\ref{annexure:hyperparameters}.  When the tree-based
benchmarks were subsequently tuned via randomised search with
cross-validation, the tuned \gls{rf} reached $R^2 = 0.9924$ and
the tuned XGBoost matched the \gls{dnn} at $R^2 = 0.9956$.  The \gls{dnn} retains
a marginal advantage ($R^2 = 0.9957$), but the practical gap is
negligible; the \gls{dnn} was retained for its differentiability rather than
for predictive superiority.  Multi-seed replication across five random
initialisations confirmed that the \gls{dnn}'s test \gls{r2} varies by only
$1.28 \times 10^{-5}$ (Table~\ref{tab:multiseed_test_performance_ch4}),
establishing that the reported performance is robust to the stochastic
training process.  This finding is consistent with the broader
health-cost prediction literature
\citep{dreweboss2022deep, samiuddin2023health, kaushik2022machine},
although the unusually high \gls{r2} reflects the clean, structured nature
of the benchmark data and should not be extrapolated to real-world
insurance portfolios.

\subsubsection*{Research Question~\ref{rq:2}}
What is the shape and distributional behaviour of the
model-implied predicted-cost distribution obtained by propagating
parametric \gls{mc} input profiles through the trained network?

The distribution is approximately symmetric, unimodal, and centred near
the mean of the observed training charges
(Figure~\ref{fig:mc_predicted_vs_actual_ch4}).  As noted in
Section~\ref{sec:mc_intro}, this close agreement is expected given the
near-perfect \gls{dnn} fit and the use of training-data marginals; the Monte
Carlo stage therefore functions primarily to define the conditioning
regions for the Shapley analysis.  Convergence diagnostics confirmed
adequate simulation size, and the multi-seed replication established
that the distributional characterisation is invariant to the network's
random initialisation (Section~\ref{sec:cross_seed_mc_ch4}).  Full
distributional summaries are reported in Chapter~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsubsection*{Research Question~\ref{rq:3}}
Is the product-marginal \gls{mc} input distribution a
defensible approximation of the training-data joint distribution for the
purpose of generating simulated profiles?

Five complementary diagnostics support an affirmative answer, including a
multivariate energy distance permutation test ($p = 0.730$) and a
Shapley attribution invariance assessment (Spearman
$\rho_{\mathrm{S}} = 1.000$).  The individual diagnostic results are
reported in Chapter~\ref{sec:results}.  It should be noted that the
independence assumption broadens the simulated input space and can
generate implausible covariate combinations; this limitation is discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:limitations_conclusion}.

\subsubsection*{Research Question~\ref{rq:4}}
How sensitive is the predicted-cost distribution to residual
perturbation from the fitted model?

The predicted-cost distribution is robust to residual perturbation.
Both empirical-residual augmentation and fixed perturbation bands
retained the same location, spread and shape.  The \gls{dnn}'s residuals on
this benchmark dataset are sufficiently small
($\hat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon} \approx \text{R}290$) that their addition
does not alter the distributional conclusions; this finding is consistent
across all five training seeds
(Section~\ref{sec:cross_seed_residual_ch4}).

\subsubsection*{Research Question~\ref{rq:5}}
Which input variables receive the largest Shapley-value
attributions globally, and how does the feature-importance ranking change
conditionally within high-cost regions of the predicted-cost distribution?

At the global level, four variables form a clear top tier: smoking status
($I_g = 0.578$), coverage level ($0.401$), medical history ($0.367$) and
family medical history ($0.351$).  Occupation ($0.171$) and exercise
frequency ($0.149$) contribute at a secondary level.  The remaining five
variables each have mean absolute Shapley values below $0.13$.  The
conditional analysis at three thresholds reveals a progressive shift.  At
$\tau_{90}$, the ranking mirrors the global ordering.  At $\tau_{95}$,
family medical history and medical history show increased conditional
importance.  At $\tau_{99}$, the ranking reverses: family medical history
($0.649$), medical history ($0.615$) and coverage level ($0.595$)
overtake smoking status ($0.563$).  The second-tier variables remain
stable across all thresholds, indicating that the reversal is driven
entirely by changes in the relative importance of the top-tier
categorical risk factors.  The multi-seed analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:cross_seed_shap}) confirmed that the global importance
ranking is perfectly preserved across all ten pairwise seed combinations
(Spearman $\rho_{\mathrm{S}} = 1.0000$), and the $\tau_{99}$ ranking
reversal is replicated in all five seeds with pairwise conditional
Spearman correlations between $0.9909$ and $1.0000$.  The SHAP
interaction analysis (Section~\ref{sec:shap_interactions}) further
established that all 55 pairwise Shapley-value correlations fall below
$|r| = 0.033$, confirming that the \gls{dnn} learned an essentially additive
mapping and that the main-effect Shapley summaries capture the full
attribution structure.

\subsubsection*{Research Question~\ref{rq:6}}
What explains any differences between the global and conditional
feature-importance rankings in the upper tail?

The ranking reversal at $\tau_{99}$ is explained by the compositional
structure of the extreme upper-tail profiles.  At $\tau_{99}$, the
proportion of smokers reaches $99\%$, the proportion of Premium-tier
policyholders reaches approximately $90\%$, and the proportion of
profiles reporting Heart disease reaches approximately $85\%$.  When
smoking status is near-universal, it contributes a near-constant positive
attribution with reduced variability and therefore ceases to
differentiate among high-cost profiles.  It is important to distinguish
two components of this effect.  The first is mechanical: a binary
feature that is near-constant within a conditioning set necessarily
produces near-constant Shapley values, regardless of any deeper
structural relationship.  This variance-collapse component is a
statistical artefact of the conditioning and would arise for any
near-degenerate binary variable.  The second component is substantive:
the monotonic increase in the conditional importance of coverage level,
medical history and family medical history across successive thresholds
is not explained by variance collapse alone, because these variables
retain meaningful variation within the $\tau_{99}$ set.  Their rising
importance reflects genuine structure in the fitted model's attribution
surface.  The residual variation in predicted costs within the
$\tau_{99}$ set is therefore attributable to coverage tier, medical
history and family medical history.
To illustrate this with a concrete example: consider two profiles in the
$\tau_{99}$ set, both smokers with high BMI.  One holds Premium coverage
with a family history of Heart disease; the other holds Standard coverage
with no family medical history.  Despite their identical smoking status,
the first profile receives substantially higher predicted costs, and the
Shapley decomposition attributes this difference to the coverage and
family history variables rather than to smoking.  The decline of
smoking status in the conditional ranking thus reflects both the
mechanical variance collapse of a near-constant feature and the
genuine explanatory role of the remaining variables.  Separating
these two components is essential before drawing broader
conclusions: the mechanical component is an inherent property of
conditional analysis on any near-degenerate variable, while the
substantive component provides information about the model's
attribution structure in the upper tail.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The dissertation makes several contributions to the intersection of
statistical learning, simulation-based inference and explainable
modelling.  Each of the three methodological components, namely deep
neural network regression, parametric \gls{mc} simulation and
Shapley-value attribution, is individually well established in the
statistical literature
\citep{vapnik1998statistical, robert2004monte, kroese2011handbook,
lundberg2017unified, molnar2022interpretable}.  The central
methodological contribution is that the conditional Shapley analysis
computed within Monte-Carlo-simulated high-cost regions produces a
form of threshold-conditional feature attribution that none of these
components can deliver in isolation.  The \gls{mc} distribution
defines the regions of interest, the Shapley decomposition reveals
the attribution structure within those regions, and the integration
creates a connected analytical framework that extends conventional
global Shapley summaries into actuarially targeted subsets of the
predicted-cost distribution.

The primary contribution is the integrated workflow itself.  In the
existing literature, these three components are typically applied in
isolation: prediction studies report out-of-sample performance metrics
without distributional analysis
\citep{samiuddin2023health, dreweboss2022deep}; \gls{mc} studies
simulate quantities of interest without connecting the results to
feature-level attribution \citep{glasserman2004monte}; and Shapley-value
studies decompose predictions on observed data without examining how the
attribution structure changes across specific regions of the
predicted-cost distribution \citep{kaushik2022machine}.
\citep{blomerus2025longterm} demonstrates the viability of deep learning
for long-term life insurance valuations, while the present study extends
the deep-learning approach to health insurance cost prediction with an
added simulation and attribution layer.  This dissertation demonstrates
that the integration produces insight that none of the three components
could provide in isolation.  The \gls{mc} distribution defines the regions of
interest for the Shapley analysis, and the conditional Shapley analysis
decomposes the model behaviour within those regions.  Each component
produces outputs that serve as inputs to the next, creating a connected
framework in which the fitted model, the simulated input population and the
feature attributions are jointly analysed.

The conditional feature-importance summaries at high-cost thresholds
represent the most distinctive contribution.  The existing health
insurance prediction literature reports global feature importance computed
on observed data only \citep{kaushik2022machine, rozemberczki2022shapley},
without conditioning attributions on specific regions of the predicted-cost
distribution.  The conditional analysis demonstrates that the variables
driving extreme predicted costs differ from those that are most important
on average, a finding that has not been documented in the health insurance
cost-prediction literature reviewed in
Chapter~\ref{sec:literature_review}.  This result contributes to the
growing literature on conditional and local interpretability methods
\citep{lundberg2017unified} by showing that global Shapley summaries can
be supplemented with threshold-specific conditional summaries to provide a
more complete picture of a model's attribution structure.

The \gls{mc} component constructs a model-implied predicted-cost
distribution from a formally stated input distribution, rather than
reporting predictions only at observed data points.  This connects the
neural network to the simulation-based inference tradition within
mathematical statistics \citep{glasserman2004monte,
rubinstein2016simulation, robert2004monte}, where the behaviour of
estimators and
predictive quantities is routinely studied through \gls{mc}
experiments.  The distinction is methodologically significant: evaluating a
model on observed data provides point predictions at covariate
combinations that happen to appear in the dataset; evaluating it on a
simulated population provides a distributional characterisation of the
model's output landscape under a specified input law, allowing the
analyst to study the range, dispersion and tail behaviour of the
predictions as statistical quantities in their own right.

The multi-seed robustness analysis represents a further contribution.
By retraining the \gls{dnn} under five independent random seeds and
replicating the entire \gls{mc} and Shapley pipeline for each seed,
the dissertation demonstrates that the predictive performance, the
model-implied cost distribution, the global Shapley ranking, and the
$\tau_{99}$ ranking reversal are all invariant to the stochastic
training process.  This type of comprehensive cross-seed replication is
not routinely performed in the applied machine-learning interpretability
literature, where results are typically reported for a single trained
network.

The multi-layered validation protocol, comprising joint-distribution
diagnostics, convergence monitoring, residual-augmented sensitivity
analysis \citep{saltelli2002making}, Shapley attribution invariance
checks and cross-seed replication, provides a template for validating
\gls{mc} and Shapley analyses in other applied settings.
These diagnostics address specific threats to validity that are not
routinely checked in the machine-learning interpretability literature,
where Shapley values are often reported without assessing their
sensitivity to the input distribution or the residual structure of the
fitted model.  Finally, the three benchmark models (Gamma \gls{glm}, \gls{rf} and
XGBoost) contextualise the \gls{dnn}'s performance across the model-class
spectrum, establishing that the predictive superiority reflects genuine
advantages of smooth nonlinear function approximation rather than
artefacts of the benchmark data.

\subsection{Strengths of the Study}
\label{sec:strengths}

The study has several methodological strengths that enhance the
reliability and reproducibility of its findings.  First, the analysis
is based on one million policyholder records, reducing sampling
variability in model fitting, hyperparameter selection and Shapley-value
estimation.  Second, the five-phase sequential hyperparameter comparison
provides a transparent, reproducible selection method documented with
validation-loss curves and selection criteria
(Annexure~\ref{annexure:hyperparameters}), avoiding the opacity of
automated search methods.  Third, strict data-leakage prevention
protocols (Section~\ref{sec:data_leakage_prevention}) ensure that all
preprocessing parameters are computed exclusively on the training
partition.  Fourth, the multi-seed replication across five random
initialisations demonstrates that neither the predictive performance nor
the attribution structure depends on the specific weight initialisation,
transforming the $\tau_{99}$ ranking reversal from a single-seed
observation into a reproducible structural property of the fitted model
class.  Finally, all conclusions are explicitly qualified as internal to
the fitted model and benchmark data, with modelling assumptions stated
formally in Table~\ref{tab:modelling_assumptions_ch3}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations_conclusion}

It is important to be forthright about the limitations that qualify the
scope of these findings.  Several of these limitations are inherent in the
methodological choices made in this dissertation; others reflect the
constraints of the benchmark data.

All results are conditional on the Kaggle Insurance Data for
Machine Learning benchmark dataset \citep{kaggleInsuranceDataML}, which has a strong simulated
structure.  The unusually high predictive performance ($R^2 = 0.9957$)
reflects this structure and should not be extrapolated to real-world
insurance portfolios, where data are noisier, relationships between
covariates and costs are more complex, and the data-generating process
may be multiplicative rather than additive.  The additive structure of the
benchmark data favours the \gls{dnn} over the log-link \gls{glm}, because the
log-link's multiplicative structure introduces systematic
misspecification.  On a dataset with a genuinely multiplicative
data-generating process, the \gls{glm}'s relative performance would likely
improve, and the \gls{dnn}'s advantage might be smaller.  The benchmark data
serves the purpose of demonstrating the methodology on a controlled,
reproducible dataset, but the specific numerical results are properties of
this dataset and not of health insurance cost prediction in general.

The study compares the \gls{dnn} against a Gamma/log-link \gls{glm}, a \gls{rf}
and an XGBoost gradient-boosted tree regressor.  While these benchmarks
span three major model classes (parametric, bagging and boosting), ensemble
methods such as stacking, Bayesian neural networks and other advanced
alternatives were not evaluated.  The existing literature suggests that
gradient-boosted trees often perform competitively with neural networks on
tabular data \citep{richman2021ai}, and indeed the XGBoost benchmark
achieves near-\gls{dnn} accuracy on this dataset.  A broader model-selection
study including additional architectures and datasets would further
strengthen the conclusions.

The \gls{mc} and Shapley analyses were initially conditional on a
single trained network with a fixed random seed.  The multi-seed
robustness analysis (Section~\ref{sec:multiseed_dnn}) subsequently
addressed this limitation by retraining the \gls{dnn} under five independent
seeds and replicating the full simulation and attribution pipeline.  The
cross-seed variability proved negligible across all stages: the test
\gls{r2} standard deviation is $1.28 \times 10^{-5}$, the \gls{mc} mean
varies by R15, and the global Shapley ranking is perfectly preserved
($\rho_{\mathrm{S}} = 1.0000$).  While five seeds do not constitute an
exhaustive exploration of the initialisation space, the convergent
results across these independent replications provide strong evidence
that the findings are not artefacts of a single random initialisation.

The tuned XGBoost benchmark achieves $R^2 = 0.9956$, matching the \gls{dnn}
($R^2 = 0.9957$) to within one unit in the fourth decimal place.  This
parity implies that the \gls{dnn}'s selection over XGBoost for the subsequent
analyses is motivated by the availability of gradient-based SHAP rather
than by a meaningful predictive advantage.  On datasets where
differentiability is not required, XGBoost may be a more parsimonious
alternative that achieves equivalent accuracy with less computational
cost.

The \gls{mc} input distribution is constructed as a product of
independent marginals.  While the joint-distribution validation confirmed
that the training-data dependence structure is weak ($p = 0.730$), the
independence assumption broadens the simulated input space beyond what
would be observed in practice.  In particular, the product-marginal
sampling can generate covariate combinations that are implausible in a
real insurance portfolio, i.e.\ profiles with extreme BMI, many dependants
and low exercise frequency that are rare or absent in the training data.
The impact of these implausible profiles on the results is mitigated by
their low probability, but it cannot be excluded entirely.

The reported attributions are gradient-based SHAP approximations, not
exact game-theoretic Shapley values.  The approximation quality depends on
the smoothness of the fitted prediction function and the adequacy of the
background sample of $m_0 = 200$ observations.  The exact Shapley values
would require evaluating the model at all $2^p$ subsets of features, which
is computationally infeasible for $p = 22$.  The gradient-based
approximation is the standard approach in the SHAP literature
\citep{lundberg2017unified}, but its approximation error has not been
quantified for this specific setting.

The conditional Shapley summaries at $\tau_{99}$ rely on approximately
1\,000 simulated profiles (the top $1\%$ of $B = 100\,000$).  The
nonparametric bootstrap confidence intervals ($R = 10\,000$ resamples)
reported in Table~\ref{tab:shap_conditional_values_ch4} provide formal
statistical support for the ranking reversal, and the monotonic trends
across $\tau_{90}$, $\tau_{95}$ and $\tau_{99}$ strengthen the finding.
Nevertheless, the precision of the conditional estimates remains lower
than the global estimates, which are computed over the full simulation.
It is worth noting that the upper-tail analysis in this dissertation is
conducted through quantile-based thresholding of the predicted-cost
distribution, rather than through the formal apparatus of \gls{evt}.  In the \gls{evt} framework, exceedances above a high threshold
are modelled using the generalised Pareto distribution, which provides a
principled basis for estimating tail probabilities and return levels.  The
conditional Shapley analysis developed here addresses a different
question, namely which input variables contribute most to predictions in
the upper tail, but it does not exploit the asymptotic distributional
theory that underpins formal tail estimation.  Future work could integrate
\gls{evt}-based tail modelling with the conditional Shapley framework by using
peaks-over-threshold methods to define the high-cost region and by
assessing the sensitivity of the attribution results to the threshold
selection.

A further methodological limitation concerns the absence of formal
uncertainty quantification around the Shapley-value estimates.  The
gradient-based SHAP implementation produces point estimates of the
attributions for each profile, but does not provide confidence intervals or
posterior distributions for the feature-importance summaries.  In a
Bayesian framework, one could place posterior distributions over the
network weights and propagate the resulting weight uncertainty through the
Shapley computation, yielding posterior distributions of the
feature-importance values.  Such an approach would complement the Monte
Carlo simulation by adding a second layer of distributional analysis:
the \gls{mc} component characterises the distribution of predicted
costs under input variability, while the Bayesian component would
characterise the distribution of Shapley attributions under parameter
uncertainty.  This extension is noted as a direction for future research
in Section~\ref{sec:future_research}.

Finally, the Shapley decomposition identifies variables to which the
fitted model assigns large contributions; it does not establish causal
relationships between input variables and healthcare expenditure.  All
interpretations are conditional on the fitted model and benchmark data,
and should not be read as causal claims about the determinants of health
insurance costs.

\subsection{Implications for Actuarial Practice and Statistical
Methodology}
\label{sec:implications}

The findings carry implications for both actuarial practice and the
broader field of statistical methodology, subject to the limitations
identified above.

\subsubsection*{Implications for actuarial practice}
The substantial performance advantage of the \gls{dnn} over the Gamma \gls{glm} on
this benchmark dataset illustrates the potential of flexible
machine-learning models to improve upon the classical pricing framework,
at least on datasets where the data-generating process does not conform to
the log-link's multiplicative structure.  The tree-based benchmarks
reinforce this conclusion: both the \gls{rf} and XGBoost
substantially outperform the \gls{glm}, with XGBoost approaching the \gls{dnn}'s
accuracy, confirming that the \gls{glm}'s limitation on this dataset is
structural rather than a general feature of simpler models.  For
actuarial practitioners, these results motivate the evaluation of
tree-based ensembles and neural networks as supplementary or alternative
pricing models alongside existing \gls{glm}-based tariffs, particularly in
settings where the relationship between covariates and costs may be
additive, nonlinear or interactive in ways that the log-link cannot
capture.  The conditional Shapley analysis has direct relevance to
actuarial risk management.  Insurance professionals are typically
interested not only in the average cost drivers across a portfolio, but
also in the variables that drive extreme costs.  The finding that the
global feature-importance ranking can be misleading in the upper tail
suggests that conditional Shapley analysis should be considered as a
standard component of model interpretation in insurance settings,
particularly when the objective is to understand the composition and risk
factors of high-cost claims.  To illustrate: a risk manager who relies
solely on the global importance ranking would focus on smoking status as
the primary intervention lever; the conditional analysis reveals that
within the extreme upper tail, the differentiating variables are coverage
tier, medical history and family medical history, which may require
different management strategies.

The \gls{mc} simulation component demonstrates a method for
constructing model-implied predicted-cost distributions from specified
input laws.  In actuarial applications, such distributions are relevant to
reserve adequacy assessment, risk capital calculations and scenario
analysis.  The ability to generate a distributional characterisation of
the model's output landscape, rather than relying on point predictions at
observed data points, provides a richer basis for actuarial decision
making.  However, these implications must be qualified by the limitations
of the benchmark data.  The unusually clean structure of the Kaggle
dataset \citep{kaggleInsuranceDataML} produces predictive performance that would be unattainable on most
real-world insurance portfolios.  The methodology is transferable, but the
specific performance figures are not.

\subsubsection*{Implications for statistical methodology}
From a statistical-methodological perspective, the dissertation
illustrates how the tools of simulation-based inference can be applied
to the outputs of fitted machine-learning models.  The construction of
a model-implied predicted-cost distribution by propagating \gls{mc}
inputs through a trained network is conceptually analogous to studying a
sampling distribution through \gls{mc} experiments.  An important
qualification applies to this particular dataset: because the input
marginals are fitted to the same data on which the network was trained,
and because the network achieves near-perfect predictive accuracy, the
model-implied distribution recovers the empirical charge distribution
almost by construction.  The \gls{mc} stage therefore serves
primarily to define the high-cost conditioning regions for the Shapley
analysis, rather than to reveal novel distributional properties.  On
datasets with more complex dependence structures, residual model error
or distribution shift between training and deployment populations, the
simulation stage would carry greater independent inferential value.
The conditional Shapley analysis extends the standard global summary by
partitioning the predicted-cost distribution into regions of interest
and examining how the attribution structure changes within those
regions.  This approach is applicable to any regression model where the
analyst is interested in the drivers of predictions within specific
output ranges, not only in insurance pricing but also in credit scoring,
healthcare resource allocation, environmental risk assessment and other
domains where the upper tail of the predicted distribution is of
particular importance.

The validation protocol provides a template for validating \gls{mc} and
Shapley analyses in other applied settings.  Joint-distribution
diagnostics check whether the input distribution introduces distributional
distortion; convergence monitoring checks whether the simulation size is
adequate; residual-augmented sensitivity analysis checks whether the
distributional conclusions are sensitive to residual variability; and
attribution invariance checks verify whether the Shapley rankings are
artefacts of the input regime.  These diagnostics address specific threats
to validity that are not routinely checked in the machine-learning
interpretability literature.

There is also a connection to the broader field of extreme value analysis.
The conditional Shapley analysis at upper-tail thresholds is conceptually
related to the peaks-over-threshold approach in \gls{evt},
where interest centres on the behaviour of a distribution above a high
quantile.  In the \gls{evt} context, the focus is on estimating tail
probabilities and return levels; in the conditional Shapley context, the
focus is on decomposing the predictions that exceed the threshold into
feature-level contributions.  The two perspectives are complementary: \gls{evt}
characterises the statistical properties of the tail, while the
conditional Shapley analysis explains the model's behaviour within the
tail.  Future work could integrate these perspectives by using
\gls{evt}-informed threshold selection to define the conditioning region for
the Shapley analysis, and by assessing whether the attribution patterns
are sensitive to the specific choice of threshold.

to the loss function so that the network's \Gls{ice} (ICE) outputs vary smoothly and monotonically with respect
to selected covariates.  Because the two studies differ in their

\textbf{Predictive performance.}
\citep{richman2024icenet} reports that the ICEnet achieves a test-set
Poisson deviance of $0.2388$, compared with $0.2387$ for the
unconstrained network, a difference of $0.0001$.  When monotonicity
constraints are applied alone, the out-of-sample deviance improves
slightly to $0.2382$, suggesting that these constraints act as a

\textbf{Interpretability.}
The ICEnet enforces actuarial reasonableness during training by
constraining the covariate-response relationships to be smooth and
monotonic, which is important for commercial pricing and regulatory
compliance.  The present dissertation pursues interpretability post hoc
through Shapley-value decomposition, revealing that the dominant cost
drivers shift between the global level and the upper tail of the
predicted-cost distribution.  This distributional insight is not
accessible through the ICE or PDP framework alone.  The two approaches
address different aspects of model interpretability and are
complementary rather than competing: constrained training ensures that
the learned relationships are commercially reasonable, while
Shapley attribution identifies which features contribute most to
individual predictions and how these contributions change in high-cost
regions.

\subsection{Recommendations for Future Research}
\label{sec:future_research}

Several directions for future research have been uncovered in the course
of this dissertation.  These can be divided into two broad categories:
extensions of the modelling and simulation components, and extensions of
the attribution and interpretation components.

\subsubsection*{Modelling and simulation extensions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Future work could extend the baseline comparison to include
          Bayesian neural networks, stacking ensembles and alternative
          deep-learning architectures (e.g.\ TabNet, FT-Transformer).
          Such comparisons would clarify whether the Shapley attribution
          patterns documented here are model-dependent or model-invariant,
          and whether more complex architectures offer further predictive
          gains beyond the \gls{dnn} and XGBoost benchmarks already evaluated.

    \item Replacing the product-marginal input distribution with a
          copula-based joint distribution would allow the simulation to
          reproduce the dependence structure of the observed data more
          faithfully.  A Gaussian copula or vine copula fitted to the
          training data would generate covariate combinations that respect
          the empirical correlation structure, eliminating the implausible
          profiles that can arise under the independence assumption.

    \item The multi-seed robustness analysis presented in
          Section~\ref{sec:multiseed_dnn} addressed the cross-seed
          sensitivity question for this benchmark dataset by retraining
          under five independent seeds.  Future work could extend this
          analysis to a larger number of seeds (e.g.\ 50 or 100) or to
          different architectures, establishing whether the observed
          cross-seed stability generalises beyond the FunnelDNN and the
          present dataset.

    \item Applying the framework to proprietary or regulatory insurance
          datasets with genuine underwriting heterogeneity would test
          whether the conditional Shapley findings generalise beyond the
          Kaggle benchmark setting \citep{kaggleInsuranceDataML}.  Real-world data would introduce
          challenges not present in the benchmark data, including missing
          values, temporal trends, policy-level effects and non-trivial
          dependence structures among covariates.

    \item Coupling the \gls{mc} simulation with Bayesian posterior
          predictive distributions or conformal prediction intervals would
          add formal uncertainty quantification to the predicted-cost
          distribution, transforming it from a point-prediction landscape
          into a set-valued prediction landscape with formal coverage
          properties.

    \item Quantile regression offers a complementary perspective on the
          conditional cost distribution that warrants investigation.
          Rather than modelling the conditional mean and subsequently
          examining quantiles of the \gls{mc} output, a quantile
          regression neural network could directly estimate conditional
          quantiles of the cost distribution at specified probability
          levels.  This approach would provide a natural alternative to
          the threshold-based conditioning used in the conditional Shapley
          analysis and would allow the analyst to study how the
          feature-importance structure varies across quantile levels
          within the fitted model itself, without the need for a separate
          \gls{mc} simulation stage.

    \item As discussed in
          Section~\ref{sec:comparison_constrained_nn}, integrating
          constrained neural-network training with the Monte
          Carlo-Shapley workflow would combine actuarial reasonableness
          with feature-level explainability.  The smoothness and
          monotonicity constraints of the ICEnet
          \citep{richman2024icenet} could be incorporated into the
          \gls{dnn}'s loss function, and Shapley-value attribution could then
          be applied to the constrained model to verify that the imposed
          constraints do not distort the feature-importance ranking in
          the upper tail.  This extension would also permit an empirical
          assessment of whether the negligible accuracy cost observed for
          frequency models extends to continuous-cost prediction.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Attribution and interpretation extensions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The $\tau_{99}$ ranking reversal could be strengthened by
          increasing the simulation size to $B = 50\,000$ or $100\,000$,
          by constructing bootstrap confidence intervals for the
          conditional mean absolute Shapley values, or by introducing
          intermediate thresholds (e.g.\ $\tau_{97.5}$, $\tau_{98}$) to
          trace the transition in the importance ranking more finely.

    \item Comparing the gradient-based SHAP attributions with alternative
          methods, such as permutation-based SHAP, KernelSHAP or LIME
          \citep{ribeiro2016should}, would assess the sensitivity of the
          results to the choice of explanation method.  Consistency across
          methods would strengthen the conclusion that the ranking
          reversal is a property of the fitted model rather than an
          artefact of the gradient-based approximation.

    \item The SHAP interaction analysis in
          Section~\ref{sec:shap_interactions} established that pairwise
          Shapley-value correlations are negligible on this benchmark
          dataset (all below $|r| = 0.033$), confirming an essentially
          additive learned mapping.  On real-world insurance portfolios
          with genuine covariate interactions, the interaction structure
          may be more complex.  Extending the interaction analysis to
          datasets with known non-additive relationships would clarify
          when main-effect Shapley summaries remain adequate and when
          higher-order interaction decompositions are necessary.

    \item The conditioning could be extended from univariate threshold
          exceedances to multivariate regions defined by combinations of
          input variables, allowing the analyst to examine how the
          attribution structure changes within specific subpopulations
          (e.g.\ elderly smokers with Premium coverage) rather than
          within quantile-based cost regions.

    \item If panel or longitudinal insurance data are available, the
          framework could be extended to study how the predicted-cost
          distribution and its Shapley decomposition evolve over time.
          Temporal changes in the feature-importance ranking would
          indicate shifts in the drivers of high-cost predictions as the
          portfolio composition changes, providing dynamic risk-management
          information that static cross-sectional analyses cannot offer.

    \item The conditional Shapley analysis could be integrated with
          formal \gls{evt} by using peaks-over-threshold
          methods and the generalised Pareto distribution to define the
          high-cost region.  \Gls{evt}-informed threshold selection would
          provide a principled statistical basis for the conditioning,
          and the resulting Shapley decomposition would be grounded in
          the asymptotic distributional theory that underpins formal tail
          estimation.  This integration would bridge the gap between
          the feature-attribution perspective developed in this
          dissertation and the tail-modelling perspective that is central
          to actuarial risk management and solvency assessment.
\end{itemize}

% 5.9 CONCLUDING REMARK

\subsection{Concluding Remark}
\label{sec:concluding_remark}

This dissertation set out to bridge two intellectual traditions that have
often developed independently: the classical statistical tradition of
studying estimators through their distributional properties, and the
modern machine-learning tradition of training flexible prediction models
and explaining their outputs.  The bridge is built from three components,
each with a well-established theoretical basis in mathematical statistics:
supervised regression provides the prediction function, \gls{mc}
simulation provides the distributional characterisation, and Shapley-value
decomposition provides the feature-level attribution.

The most noteworthy finding is the feature-importance ranking reversal at
the 99th percentile of the predicted-cost distribution.  While smoking
status is the dominant contributor to predicted costs globally, it loses
its discriminating power in the extreme upper tail, where virtually all
profiles are smokers.  Within this tail, the variables that differentiate
the costliest profiles from one another are coverage level, medical
history and family medical history.  The multi-seed replication across
five independent random initialisations confirms that this reversal is a
robust structural property of the fitted model class rather than an
artefact of a single training run, lending additional confidence to the
finding.  This result illustrates a general principle that extends well
beyond insurance pricing: a variable's importance in a statistical model
is not a fixed property, but depends on the region of the output space
under consideration.  Global importance summaries, while useful, can
obscure the attribution structure within subpopulations of particular
interest.

The framework developed here is not specific to health insurance or to the
particular benchmark dataset analysed.  The three-stage framework of
prediction, simulation and conditional explanation is applicable to any
regression problem where the analyst seeks to understand the distributional
behaviour of a model's predictions and to decompose those predictions into
feature-level contributions within specific regions of the output space.
The validation diagnostics developed for the \gls{mc} and Shapley
components provide a reusable template for assessing the reliability of
such analyses in other applied settings, from credit scoring and
healthcare resource allocation to environmental risk assessment and
financial modelling.

The development of this framework reflects a conviction that the tools of
mathematical statistics remain essential for understanding and interpreting
the outputs of modern machine-learning models.  Neural networks are
powerful prediction devices, but their value to the applied statistician
extends beyond the point predictions they produce.  By treating the trained
network as a deterministic transformation to be studied through \gls{mc}
experiments and decomposed through Shapley values, this dissertation
connects the machine-learning prediction paradigm to the classical
statistical tradition of studying estimators through their distributional
properties.  While this connection may seem like a modest step within the
vast landscape of statistical learning and explainable modelling, it is
worth remembering that each contribution to the field of interpretable
machine learning is, in its own way, a contribution to the broader
enterprise of understanding how statistical models learn from data, what
they learn, and where their learned representations succeed or fail in
capturing the structure of the phenomena they are designed to predict.
