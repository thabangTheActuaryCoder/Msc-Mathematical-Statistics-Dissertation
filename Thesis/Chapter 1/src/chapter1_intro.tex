% 1.1 BACKGROUND AND PROBLEM STATEMENT

\subsection{Background and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:background_problem_statement}

Health insurance cost prediction lies at the intersection of statistical
learning, actuarial modelling and explainable machine learning.  The
empirical task involves estimating a continuous monetary outcome from
policyholder characteristics such as age, \gls{bmi}, smoking
status, coverage level and medical history.  In the insurance industry,
these estimates underpin individual pricing, reserve adequacy and
portfolio risk management.  A single fitted value or prediction-error
metric, however, does not fully characterise the behaviour of the fitted
model.  Understanding how predicted costs vary across a simulated
population and identifying which input variables contribute most to high
predicted costs are questions that require methods beyond point
prediction.

It is therefore important to establish the modelling landscape within
which this dissertation operates: the classical actuarial framework
based on \glspl{glm}, the tree-based ensemble methods that
occupy an intermediate position between linear and fully flexible models,
the introduction of \glspl{dnn} into insurance pricing, and the
growing demand for post-hoc model explanation.  Each of these themes is
introduced below, before the specific research problem and analytical
framework are stated.

\subsubsection{Classical actuarial pricing models}
\label{sec:classical_actuarial}

The statistical modelling of insurance costs has a long history.
\Glspl{glm} have served as the standard framework
for actuarial pricing since the seminal work of
\citep{nelder1972generalized} and their subsequent adoption by the
insurance profession.  In health insurance, the Gamma distribution with
a logarithmic link function is commonly used to model the right-skewed,
positive-valued distribution of claim amounts.  The Gamma family is
well suited to this setting because it is supported on the positive
real line, matching the strictly positive nature of insurance costs,
and its variance increases with the mean, accommodating the greater
dispersion typically observed among larger claims.  The logarithmic
link function complements this distributional choice by ensuring that
predicted mean costs remain strictly positive and by inducing a
multiplicative tariff structure in which each covariate enters as a
proportional adjustment to the base cost.  Under this specification,
the conditional mean of the response is given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:glm_log_link_intro}
\log \mu_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\beta},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i = E[Y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i]$ is the conditional mean cost
for policyholder $i$, $\mathbf{x}_i$ is the vector of covariate values
and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is the coefficient vector.  The multiplicative
structure of the log-link allows each coefficient to be interpreted as a
relative risk factor: exponentiating $\beta_j$ yields the multiplicative
effect of a unit increase in the $j$-th covariate on the expected cost,
holding all other covariates constant.  This interpretive simplicity has
made \glspl{glm} the dominant modelling tool in actuarial practice for several
decades.

Despite their advantages, \glspl{glm} impose a pre-specified linear predictor
structure that restricts the functional form of the relationship between
covariates and the response.  Interaction effects must be specified
manually, and nonlinear relationships can only be captured through
explicit basis expansions or spline terms.  When the true relationship
between policyholder attributes and cost is complex, involving
higher-order interactions among multiple categorical and continuous
variables, these structural constraints limit the predictive performance
of the model.  The gap between what a linear predictor can represent and
what the data contain motivates the exploration of more flexible model
classes.

A further limitation concerns the distributional assumption itself.
The Gamma distribution implies a specific variance-mean relationship
($\text{Var}(Y_i) \propto \mu_i^2$), which may not hold uniformly
across all segments of the policyholder population.  While robust standard errors and alternative
distributional assumptions can mitigate this concern, the fundamental
limitation of the linear predictor structure remains.  As
\citep{goodfellow2016deep} note, the capacity of a model class to
approximate a target function is determined by the richness of the
hypothesis space, and the hypothesis space of a \gls{glm} is inherently
constrained by its additive structure.

Between the constrained linear predictor of the \gls{glm} and the fully
flexible hidden-layer representations of a \gls{dnn} lie
tree-based ensemble methods, which approximate nonlinear relationships
through collections of decision trees.  \Glspl{rf}
\citep{breiman2001random} aggregate predictions across independently
grown trees, each trained on a bootstrap sample with a random feature
subset at each split, reducing variance while capturing interactions
that the \gls{glm} cannot represent.  Gradient-boosted trees, exemplified by
the XGBoost implementation \citep{chen2016xgboost}, construct an
additive ensemble of shallow trees sequentially, with each new tree
fitted to the residuals of the preceding ensemble.  Both methods offer
greater flexibility than the \gls{glm} but remain fundamentally
piecewise-constant approximators: unlike neural networks, they do not
learn smooth, continuous mappings through differentiable transformations.
Including a \gls{rf} and an XGBoost benchmark alongside the \gls{glm} and
\gls{dnn} provides a graded comparison across model classes of increasing
flexibility.  The core comparison remains the actuarial-standard \gls{glm}
against the \gls{dnn}, since this dissertation is situated within an actuarial
modelling context where the \gls{glm} is the established industry baseline and
the \gls{dnn} represents the class of model whose behaviour the \gls{mc}
and Shapley analyses are designed to examine.  The tree-based benchmarks
complement this pairing by establishing where piecewise-constant
nonlinear methods fall on the performance spectrum.

\subsubsection{\Glspl{dnn} for insurance regression}
\label{sec:dnn_intro}

\Glspl{dnn} provide a flexible alternative to the \gls{glm} framework.
The universal approximation theorem
\citep{hornik1989multilayer, cybenko1989approximation} establishes that a
sufficiently wide feed-forward network with a non-polynomial activation
function can approximate any continuous function on a compact set to
arbitrary precision.  In formal terms, for any continuous
$f: [0,1]^p \to \mathbb{R}$ and any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a
single-hidden-layer network $F$ such that
$\sup_{\mathbf{x}} |F(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{x})| < \varepsilon$.
The domain $[0,1]^p$ requires that every input variable is confined
to a finite, closed range.  The specific choice of $[0,1]$ rather
than some other interval is a notational convention: any bounded range
$[a,b]$ can be mapped to $[0,1]$ by a simple linear rescaling, so the
theorem applies equally to any compact input domain.  What matters is
not the particular endpoints but the fact that the inputs cannot grow
without bound; if they could, no fixed-size network would be able to
track the target function everywhere.  In this dissertation the
condition is met naturally: all numerical inputs are rescaled to a
bounded interval during preprocessing and all categorical inputs are
coded as zero-one indicators, so the network always operates within a
bounded input space.  In
practice, modern deep networks with multiple hidden layers and nonlinear
activations learn hierarchical representations that capture complex
interactions without requiring the modeller to enumerate them in advance
\citep{goodfellow2016deep}.

This capacity for automatic feature interaction has led to increasing
adoption of neural networks in insurance applications.  Studies by
\citep{richman2021ai} and \citep{kuo2019deep} have demonstrated that
neural networks can achieve predictive improvements over classical
actuarial models on insurance cost prediction tasks, particularly when
the data contain nonlinear covariate-response relationships that the \gls{glm}
cannot capture.  The feed-forward architecture, in which each layer
computes $\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)} = \sigma(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}
\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)})$ for an activation function
$\sigma$, weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}$ and bias vector
$\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}$, is the most commonly used architecture for
tabular regression problems.

The flexibility of \glspl{dnn}, however, comes at the expense of
direct interpretability.  The parameters of a trained network, which may
number in the tens of thousands, do not admit the same coefficient-level
reading as a linear model.  A policyholder with specific attributes
receives a predicted cost, but the relative contribution of each
attribute to that prediction is not immediately transparent from the
network weights.  This lack of transparency is a practical concern in
regulated industries such as insurance, where stakeholders require not
only accurate predictions but also explanations of the factors driving
those predictions.  The tension between predictive flexibility and model
transparency has motivated a substantial body of research on post-hoc
explanation methods, of which Shapley-value attribution is one of the
most principled.

\subsubsection{Beyond point prediction: distributional analysis and
feature attribution}
\label{sec:beyond_point_prediction}

Prediction accuracy alone provides an incomplete picture of model
behaviour.  A test-set metric such as \gls{r2} or \gls{rmse} summarises average
predictive performance but does not reveal how the predicted cost varies
across the input space, what the distributional shape of predicted costs
looks like under population-level input variation, or which variables are
most important in specific regions of the output distribution.  Answering
these questions requires supplementary analyses that go beyond the
standard train-test evaluation paradigm.

The challenge is compounded by the heterogeneity of policyholder
populations.  An insurance portfolio typically contains a large number of
low-cost policyholders and a smaller number of high-cost policyholders
whose claims dominate the aggregate loss.  From a risk-management
perspective, understanding the drivers of predicted costs in this upper
tail is at least as important as understanding the average prediction.
Global feature-importance summaries, which aggregate attributions across
all predictions, may obscure the factors that are most relevant in the
high-cost region.  A conditional analysis that focuses specifically on
the upper tail of the predicted-cost distribution is therefore needed
to complement the global view.

This tension between predictive flexibility and interpretability, and
between point-prediction accuracy and distributional understanding,
motivates the present study.  A framework is needed that combines
flexible prediction with structured distributional analysis and
principled feature attribution, so that the fitted model can be
examined not only for its accuracy but also for its behaviour under
simulated input variation and for the variables to which it assigns
large contributions in the upper tail of predicted costs.

\subsection{Positioning within Mathematical Statistics}
\label{sec:positioning}

This dissertation is positioned within statistical learning,
simulation-based inference, model-implied predictive distributions, and
explainable statistical modelling.  The statistical contribution is not
only the fitting of a predictive model, but the construction of a
model-implied distribution under an explicit input law and the
conditional analysis of feature attributions in the upper tail of that
distribution.  Each of the three methodological pillars is discussed
below to situate the work within the broader mathematical-statistics
tradition.

\subsubsection{Supervised regression and function approximation}
\label{sec:supervised_regression_intro}

The predictive component draws on the theory of supervised regression and
neural-network function approximation.  Supervised regression seeks a
function $\hat{f}: \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}$ that minimises a loss
functional, typically the expected squared error
$E[(Y - \hat{f}(\mathbf{X}))^2]$, over a distribution of input-output
pairs $(\mathbf{X}, Y)$.  The \gls{dnn} is one particular
parameterisation of this function class, distinguished by its capacity to
represent highly nonlinear mappings through compositions of affine
transformations and elementwise activation functions.

The bias-variance decomposition provides the theoretical framework for
understanding the trade-off between model complexity and generalisation
performance.  A model that is too simple (high bias) will underfit the
data, while a model that is too complex (high variance) will overfit.
The five-phase hyperparameter comparison employed in this dissertation is
designed to navigate this trade-off by selecting the configuration that
minimises validation-set error, while the held-out test set provides an
unbiased estimate of the generalisation performance.

\subsubsection{\gls{mc} simulation in statistical inference}
\label{sec:mc_intro}

The simulation component uses parametric \gls{mc} methods, one of the
foundational tools of computational statistics.  \gls{mc} simulation
allows the analyst to explore the distribution of a quantity of interest
by drawing random samples from a specified input distribution and
propagating them through a deterministic or stochastic transformation.
In the classical setting, \gls{mc} methods are used to study the
sampling distribution of estimators, the power of test statistics and the
coverage properties of confidence intervals.

In this dissertation, the transformation is the trained neural network
prediction function $\hat{f}$, and the input distribution is a product of
parametric marginals fitted to the training data.  The resulting
distribution of predicted costs is a model-implied distribution that
characterises the range, central tendency, dispersion and shape of the
network's output under the specified input law.  Formally, if
$\mathbf{X} \sim F_{\mathbf{X}}$ denotes the input distribution and
$\hat{Y} = \hat{f}(\mathbf{X})$ denotes the predicted cost, then the
distribution of $\hat{Y}$ under $F_{\mathbf{X}}$ is the object of
inferential interest.  This construction draws on the simulation-based
inference tradition within mathematical statistics, where the behaviour
of estimators, test statistics and predictive quantities is routinely
studied through \gls{mc} experiments.  A caveat is warranted: because
the marginals are fitted to the same data on which the network was
trained, and because the network achieves near-perfect fit on this
benchmark dataset, the model-implied predicted-cost distribution
necessarily recovers the empirical charge distribution almost by
construction.  The primary role of the \gls{mc} stage in this
application is therefore not to reveal unexpected distributional
properties, but to define the high-cost conditioning regions on which
the Shapley analysis operates.

\subsubsection{Shapley values and cooperative game theory}
\label{sec:shapley_intro}

The explanation component applies Shapley-value decomposition, a concept
originating in cooperative game theory \citep{shapley1953value}, to
attribute fitted predictions to individual input variables.  In a
cooperative game with $p$ players and a characteristic function $v$ that
maps each coalition $S \subseteq \{1, \ldots, p\}$ to a real-valued
payoff, the Shapley value of player $j$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:shapley_intro}
\phi_j(v) = \sum_{S \subseteq \{1,\ldots,p\} \setminus \{j\}}
\frac{|S|!\,(p - |S| - 1)!}{p!}
\bigl[v(S \cup \{j\}) - v(S)\bigr].
\end{equation}
The Shapley value provides the unique additive decomposition of the
game's total payoff that satisfies the axioms of efficiency, symmetry,
linearity and the null-player property.

In the machine-learning interpretability literature, this decomposition
has been operationalised through the SHAP framework
\citep{lundberg2017unified}, which interprets each prediction as a
cooperative game in which the ``players'' are input features and the
``payoff'' is the predicted value.  Among the available approximation strategies, a gradient-based
implementation is adopted in this dissertation because it exploits the
differentiability of the neural network to compute Shapley-value
estimates without repeated model re-evaluation, making it
computationally tractable for the large simulated samples considered
here.  Permutation-based alternatives, while model-agnostic, require
a combinatorial number of forward passes that would be prohibitive at
the sample sizes used in this analysis.

\subsubsection{The integration as the statistical contribution}
\label{sec:integration_contribution}

The choice to combine these three components reflects a broader
methodological perspective: that a fitted prediction model is a
statistical object whose properties extend beyond its point-prediction
accuracy.  Just as the sampling distribution of an estimator is studied
through \gls{mc} experiments to understand its variability, the
distribution of a prediction function's outputs under a specified input
law can be studied to understand the range and shape of its predictions.
And just as the influence of individual observations on an estimator can
be decomposed through influence functions, the contribution of
individual input variables to a prediction can be decomposed through
Shapley values.

Each of these components has a well-established theoretical basis in
mathematical statistics.  The contribution of this dissertation lies not
in advancing any single component, but in their integration within a
single empirical workflow applied to health insurance cost prediction.
The \gls{mc} distribution defines the regions of interest for the
Shapley analysis, and the conditional Shapley analysis decomposes the
model behaviour within those regions.  This integration produces insight
that none of the three components could provide in isolation.

\subsection{Conceptual framework}
\label{sec:conceptual_framework}

The analytical workflow of this dissertation is organised around three
sequentially connected stages: prediction, simulation and explanation.
Each stage produces outputs that serve as inputs to the next, creating an
integrated framework in which the fitted model, the simulated input
population and the feature attributions are jointly analysed.

In the first stage, a \gls{dnn} regression model is trained on
the benchmark dataset using a systematic hyperparameter comparison.  The
trained network defines a prediction function
$\hat{f}: \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}$ that maps encoded policyholder
attributes to predicted costs.  A Gamma \gls{glm} with a
logarithmic link, a \gls{rf} regressor and an XGBoost
gradient-boosted tree regressor are fitted alongside the network to
provide benchmarks spanning three alternative model classes.  The
primary comparison is between the actuarial-standard \gls{glm} and the \gls{dnn},
with the tree-based models serving as intermediate reference points.
The output of this stage is a validated prediction function with
documented out-of-sample performance across all four models.

In the second stage, parametric distributions are fitted to each input
variable in the training partition, and a \gls{mc} sample of
$B = 100\,000$ synthetic policyholder profiles is drawn from the product
of these marginals.  Each profile is encoded and passed through the
trained prediction function, yielding a sample of $B$ predicted costs.
This sample constitutes a model-implied predicted-cost distribution
whose properties, including mean, variance, skewness and tail behaviour,
are analysed through summary statistics, density estimation and
convergence diagnostics.  Joint-distribution validation and
residual-augmented sensitivity analysis confirm the adequacy of the input
distribution and the robustness of the results.

In the third stage, gradient-based Shapley values are computed for each
of the $B$ simulated profiles.  These values decompose each predicted
cost into additive contributions from the $p$ input variables.  The
Shapley values are summarised at the global level (across all profiles)
and conditionally within high-cost regions defined by upper-tail
thresholds at the 90th, 95th and 99th percentiles.  The conditional
analysis reveals how the feature-importance ranking changes as the
focus shifts from average to extreme predicted costs.

This three-stage structure ensures that the dissertation answers not only
``How well does the model predict?'' but also ``What does the model's
output distribution look like under population-level input variation?''
and ``Which variables matter most, and does their relative importance
change in the tail?''

\subsection{Significance of the Study}
\label{sec:significance}

The study contributes to several intersecting areas within mathematical
statistics and actuarial science.

From a methodological perspective, the integration of \gls{dnn}
prediction, parametric \gls{mc} simulation and Shapley-value
attribution within a single reproducible framework demonstrates a workflow
that is applicable to any regression problem where distributional
analysis and feature attribution in specific output regions are of
interest.  While each component is individually well established, their
combination in a sequential framework, where the simulation defines the
regions and the Shapley analysis explains the model behaviour within
those regions, is not standard in the literature.

From a practical perspective, the conditional Shapley analysis at
upper-tail thresholds addresses a question of direct relevance to
actuarial practice: which variables drive the most expensive predicted
claims?  Traditional global feature-importance summaries may mask the
fact that the drivers of average-cost predictions differ from the
drivers of extreme-cost predictions.  By computing conditional Shapley
summaries at multiple thresholds, the study provides a graded view of
how feature importance evolves into the tail.  This information is
relevant to risk selection, pricing segmentation and reinsurance
analysis, where the focus is often on the upper tail of the cost
distribution.

From a statistical-learning perspective, the construction of a
model-implied predicted-cost distribution under an explicit input law
connects the neural network prediction function to the broader tradition
of simulation-based inference.  Rather than evaluating the model only at
observed data points, the \gls{mc} component generates a distribution
of predicted costs that reveals the range and shape of the model's
output landscape.  This approach treats the prediction function as a
statistical object whose distributional properties are themselves worthy
of analysis, consistent with the mathematical-statistics emphasis on
characterising estimators and prediction rules through their sampling
distributions.

Finally, from an explainability perspective, the study demonstrates that
Shapley-value attribution, often presented as a single global summary,
can be extended to conditional summaries that provide region-specific
explanations.  The ranking reversal observed in the upper tail of the
predicted-cost distribution illustrates that a single global importance
ranking may not adequately represent the model's behaviour in all
regions of the output space.  This finding contributes to the growing
literature on context-dependent model explanation.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research_questions}

The background and problem statement outlined above motivate a set of
research questions that span the three stages of the analytical
framework.  These questions are formulated to be answerable within the
scope of the benchmark data and the fitted model, without requiring
causal interpretation.  Each question addresses a specific aspect of the
prediction, simulation and explanation framework.

The dissertation addresses the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \label{rq:1} How does a \gls{dnn} regression model compare with a
          classical \gls{glm}, a \gls{rf} and an
          XGBoost gradient-boosted tree in predicting health insurance
          costs on a benchmark dataset?

    \item \label{rq:2} What is the shape and distributional behaviour of the
          model-implied predicted-cost distribution obtained by propagating
          parametric \gls{mc} input profiles through the trained network?

    \item \label{rq:3} Is the product-marginal \gls{mc} input distribution a
          defensible approximation of the training-data joint distribution
          for the purpose of generating simulated profiles?

    \item \label{rq:4} How sensitive is the predicted-cost distribution to
          residual perturbation from the fitted model?

    \item \label{rq:5} Which input variables receive the largest Shapley-value
          attributions globally, and how does the feature-importance
          ranking change conditionally within high-cost regions of the
          predicted-cost distribution?

    \item \label{rq:6} What explains any differences between the global and
          conditional feature-importance rankings in the upper tail?
\end{enumerate}

Research Question~\ref{rq:1} addresses the predictive stage.  Research
Questions~\ref{rq:2} to \ref{rq:4} address the simulation stage, covering the distributional
characterisation, the input-distribution validity and the
residual-perturbation robustness respectively.  Research Questions~\ref{rq:5}
and~\ref{rq:6} address the explanation stage, covering both the global and
conditional Shapley analyses and the interpretation of any observed
ranking changes.

\subsection{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:research_objectives}

The research questions stated above are operationalised through the
following objectives, each of which maps directly to one or more research
questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To fit a \gls{dnn} regression model to a health
          insurance benchmark dataset and to evaluate its predictive
          performance against a classical \gls{glm}, a
          \gls{rf} and an XGBoost gradient-boosted tree benchmark.

    \item To design and implement a parametric \gls{mc} simulation
          that generates synthetic policyholder profiles from fitted
          marginal distributions, propagates them through the trained
          network, and produces a model-implied distribution of
          predicted costs.

    \item To validate the \gls{mc} input distribution against the
          observed training data using joint-distribution diagnostics,
          bootstrap resampling, and a Shapley attribution invariance
          check.

    \item To conduct a residual-augmented \gls{mc} sensitivity
          analysis that examines the effect of residual perturbation on
          the predicted-cost distribution.

    \item To compute Shapley-value attributions for simulated
          policyholder profiles and to summarise feature importance at
          the global level and conditionally within high-cost regions
          defined by upper-tail thresholds.

    \item To examine how the conditional feature-importance ranking in
          high-cost regions differs from the global ranking, and to
          interpret these differences within the context of the fitted
          model and benchmark data.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Validation and Robustness Strategy}
\label{sec:validation_strategy}

A central concern in any modelling study is whether the reported results
are genuine reflections of the model's behaviour or artefacts of data
contamination, distributional mismatch or estimation instability.  This
dissertation addresses this concern through a multi-layered validation
strategy that spans all three stages of the analytical framework.

At the prediction stage, the validation strategy consists of three
elements.  First, a strict data-leakage prevention protocol ensures that
all preprocessing parameters, including means, standard deviations,
category mappings and normalisation constants, are computed exclusively
on the training partition and applied without refitting to the validation
and test partitions.  This guarantees that the model has no access to
information from the evaluation data during training.  Second, the
five-phase hyperparameter comparison uses validation-set performance as
the selection criterion, while the final performance metrics are reported
on the held-out test set that is never used for model selection.  Third,
the comparison of training-set and test-set performance metrics for both
the \gls{dnn} and the \gls{glm} provides evidence on the presence or absence of
overfitting.

At the simulation stage, the validation strategy addresses the central
question of whether the independent-marginal \gls{mc} input
distribution is a defensible approximation of the training-data joint
distribution.  This is assessed through four complementary diagnostics:
pairwise correlation comparisons, categorical cross-tabulation
comparisons, PCA projection overlays, and a formal energy distance
permutation test.  In addition, convergence diagnostics monitor the
stability of the predicted-cost distribution summary statistics as the
simulation size increases, confirming that $B = 100\,000$ is sufficient
for the level of \gls{mc} precision required.  The
residual-augmented sensitivity analysis adds a further robustness layer
by examining whether the distributional conclusions change when scaled
fitted residuals are incorporated into the predicted costs.

At the explanation stage, the validation strategy uses a Shapley
attribution invariance check.  This diagnostic compares the
feature-importance rankings obtained when Shapley values are computed on
training-data input profiles with those obtained on \gls{mc} input
profiles.  Agreement between the two regimes confirms that the Shapley
results are not sensitive to the choice of input regime and that the
\gls{mc} input distribution produces attributions consistent with
those obtained from observed data.

The details of each validation component are documented in the methods
chapter (Chapter~\ref{chap:methods_and_data}), and the corresponding
results are reported in Chapter~\ref{sec:results}.

% 1.8 SCOPE AND DELIMITATIONS
% ---------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Scope and Delimitations}
\label{sec:scope_delimitations}

The following delimitations define the boundaries of the study:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benchmark data.}  The analysis uses the Kaggle
          Insurance Data for Machine Learning benchmark
          \citep{kaggleInsuranceDataML}, a publicly
          available benchmark dataset containing one million policyholder
          records.  The dataset was generated by sampling eleven
          predictor variables (age, sex, BMI, number of children,
          smoking status, region, exercise frequency, occupation,
          coverage level, medical history and family medical history)
          from pre-specified distributions and computing the response
          variable (insurance charges) through a deterministic formula
          that combines these predictors with fixed coefficients and
          interaction terms.  Because the response is constructed
          algorithmically rather than observed from real claims, the
          covariate-response relationship is largely noise-free, which
          is why the fitted models achieve unusually high predictive
          accuracy.  The results describe relationships learned by the
          fitted model within this benchmark setting and should not be
          interpreted as causal determinants of healthcare costs or as
          direct evidence of real-world pricing practice.  The
          performance figures should not be extrapolated to real-world
          insurance portfolios, where data are noisier and
          covariate-response relationships are more complex.

    \item \textbf{Focused model comparison.}  Three benchmark models (a
          Gamma \gls{glm}, a \gls{rf} and an XGBoost gradient-boosted
          tree) are included to contextualise the predictive performance
          of the \gls{dnn} across model classes of increasing
          flexibility.  However, the dissertation does not attempt an
          exhaustive model-selection study.  Bayesian methods, support
          vector machines, other neural-network architectures and
          stacked-ensemble approaches are not evaluated.  The comparison
          is designed to position the \gls{dnn} within a spectrum from linear
          to piecewise-constant to smooth nonlinear models, rather than
          to identify the globally optimal model class.

    \item \textbf{Single fitted network.}  The \gls{mc} and Shapley
          analyses are applied to a single trained network with a fixed
          random seed.  The predicted-cost distribution and feature
          attributions are conditional on this particular fitted model and
          its estimated parameters.  Variability across random
          initialisations is not quantified.

    \item \textbf{Independent marginals.}  The \gls{mc} input
          distribution is constructed as a product of independent fitted
          marginals.  This is a deliberate modelling choice that
          broadens the simulated input space but does not reproduce
          the dependence structure of the observed data.  Copula-based
          joint distributions are not considered.

    \item \textbf{Approximate Shapley values.}  The reported
          attributions are gradient-based SHAP approximations, not
          exact game-theoretic Shapley values.  They are conditional on
          the fitted network, the background sample, and the explainer
          settings.  Exact Shapley computation is infeasible for the
          number of features and the sample size considered.

    \item \textbf{No causal inference.}  The Shapley decomposition
          identifies variables to which the fitted model assigns large
          contributions.  It does not establish causal relationships
          between input variables and healthcare expenditure.  All
          attributions are internal to the fitted model and the benchmark
          data.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethical_considerations}

The analysis is conducted on the Kaggle Insurance Data for
Machine Learning benchmark dataset \citep{kaggleInsuranceDataML}, which is publicly available and
contains no personally identifiable information.  The dataset does not represent actual policyholder records
from any insurance company.  No data collection involving human
participants was conducted for this study, and no ethical clearance from
a research ethics committee was required.

Notwithstanding the use of benchmark data, the dissertation acknowledges
the broader ethical considerations associated with the use of machine
learning in insurance pricing.  Predictive models that use sensitive
attributes such as gender, age or health status as input variables may
raise fairness concerns if deployed in production pricing systems.  The
Shapley-value analysis presented in this dissertation quantifies the
contribution of each variable to the fitted model's predictions, which
could in principle inform fairness audits by identifying variables that
exert disproportionate influence on predicted costs.  However, the study
does not conduct a formal fairness analysis, and the Shapley
attributions should not be interpreted as evidence of discriminatory
pricing.

All modelling code, trained model artefacts and simulation outputs are
documented to support reproducibility.  The use of a fixed random seed
throughout the framework ensures that all results can be replicated by
independent researchers with access to the same dataset and software
environment.

\subsection{Dissertation Outline}
\label{sec:dissertation_outline}

The remainder of this dissertation is organised as follows.

\subsubsection*{Chapter~\ref{sec:literature_review}: Literature Review}
This chapter reviews the theoretical and empirical foundations of the
\gls{dnn}, \gls{mc} and Shapley framework.  The review covers statistical
learning theory and supervised regression, \gls{dnn}
architectures and their approximation properties, \glspl{glm} as actuarial baselines, health insurance cost prediction with
machine learning, \gls{mc} simulation methodology, Shapley-value
attribution theory and the SHAP framework, and conditional
feature-importance analysis.  The chapter concludes with a formal
identification of the research gap that motivates the present study.

\subsubsection*{Chapter~\ref{chap:methods_and_data}: Methods and Data}
This chapter describes the benchmark dataset, the data preprocessing and
encoding procedure, the exploratory data analysis, the network
architecture and training protocol, the five-phase hyperparameter
comparison, the predictive and structural diagnostics, the \gls{glm} and
tree-based benchmark specifications, the \gls{mc} simulation design
and input-distribution validation, the residual-augmented sensitivity
analysis, and the Shapley-value implementation.  All methodological
choices are documented with their statistical justification, and a
consolidated table of modelling assumptions is provided.

\subsubsection*{Chapter~\ref{sec:results}: Results}
This chapter presents the empirical results of the three-stage workflow.
The chapter reports the hyperparameter comparison outcomes, the
four-model benchmark comparison (\gls{glm}, \gls{rf}, XGBoost and \gls{dnn}),
the \gls{mc} predicted-cost distribution and its convergence
diagnostics, the joint-distribution validation results, the
residual-augmented sensitivity analysis, the global and conditional
Shapley-value attributions, the feature-importance ranking reversal in
the upper tail, and the Shapley attribution invariance check.

\subsubsection*{Chapter~\ref{sec:conclusion}: Conclusion}
The final chapter summarises the key findings in relation to each
research question, states the contributions and strengths of the study,
discusses limitations, examines implications for actuarial practice and
statistical methodology, and identifies directions for future research.
