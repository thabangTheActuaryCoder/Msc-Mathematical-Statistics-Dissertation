\section*{Abstract}

This dissertation develops a three-stage statistical framework for predicting health insurance costs, using a publicly available benchmark dataset of one million policyholder records.
In the first stage, a deep neural network is trained to predict insurance charges.  Its accuracy is compared against three established statistical models through a structured selection process that evaluates different network configurations.
In the second stage, computer simulation generates a large population of realistic but synthetic policyholder profiles based on the patterns observed in the data.  The trained network then predicts costs for each simulated profile, producing a full distribution of predicted costs that is checked for consistency with the original data.
In the third stage, each predicted cost is broken down into the individual contributions of policyholder characteristics such as age, smoking status and coverage level, both across the full population and specifically among the highest-cost profiles.

The deep neural network captures over 99\% of the variation in insurance costs, outperforming all three benchmark models.
The contribution analysis identifies smoking status, coverage level and medical history as the strongest overall cost drivers.
Among the highest-cost profiles, however, the ranking changes: coverage level, medical history and family medical history become more important than smoking status.  This shift shows that the factors driving extreme predicted costs are not the same as those most important on average.
All conclusions relate to the fitted model and benchmark data and are not presented as causal claims.
